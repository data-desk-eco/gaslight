Undeclared flaring in the Permian Basin

Are operators flaring without declaring it? Satellite flare observations — VIIRS Nightfire and Sentinel-2 — cross-matched against the flaring volumes operators declare to the Railroad Commission of Texas say yes: roughly a quarter of all sustained satellite-observed flare activity at producing leases has zero flared gas declared for the month in question.

The headline numbers

For each of 1,297 VIIRS flare sites we collected every lease with a well inside 375 m — half a VIIRS pixel — and summed the flaring those leases declared to the RRC for each month the satellite saw fire on three or more nights. Sites within 1 km of a gas processing plant are excluded: plant flaring is legitimate non-lease reporting, and the plant list unions the RRC’s R-3 facilities with the EIA-757 plant survey, plus an imagery review of every named case.

In 3,034 site-months a flare burned for at least three nights while every lease within the pixel reported gas production but not one MCF flared. At the median declared-flaring rate of ~730 MCF per MW of observed radiant heat, that implies very roughly ~11.5 BCF of flared gas over five years that never appears in RRC disposition records — an order of magnitude, not an estimate.

Pioneer Natural Resources · Glasscock County

The Arnett lease

30undeclared months
385flare-nights
907MW radiant heat

The biggest undeclared signal in the basin. The flare at lease 08-023557 burned 132–217 nights every year from 2021 to 2025, yet the lease reported production of just 0–3 MMCF a year and a single 63 MCF declaration, in 2023. Carbon Mapper imaged methane plumes of 247–732 kg/hr nearby six times in 2024–25.

Either the flare burns gas that never makes the production report, or the production report is itself suspect. Pioneer — now part of ExxonMobil — is also #1 in the single-operator rollup: 86 undeclared months and 1,457 MW across 34 sites.

Oxyrock Operating · Glasscock County

Jones “A”: a flare and a methane source

42undeclared months
877MCF declared in 5 yrs
~2,500kg/hr peak plume

The most persistent case: 42 undeclared months while production held steady at ~150 MMCF a year and declarations totalled 877 MCF over five years. The fire grew from 20 nights in 2021 to 136 nights in 2025; Sentinel-2 sees it at 0.69 persistence, 98 m away.

Carbon Mapper has imaged methane plumes within 200 m of this site repeatedly since 2019, at rates up to ~2,500 kg/hr — a flare and a chronic methane source, with essentially nothing on the books.

Anadarko / Oxy · Loving County

The flare that never existed

44undeclared months
466flare-nights
0MCF ever declared

Nothing has ever been declared at this site. The flare burned through every year from 2021 to 2025 while the matched lease produced 277–1,041 MMCF a year, and escalated sharply in 2024 — 174 nights and 347 MW. Methane plumes were imaged nearby in 2021 (158 kg/hr) and 2025 (439 kg/hr).

Anadarko also tops the frequency rollup: 211 undeclared months across 42 single-operator sites — high frequency, low intensity.

XTO Energy · Martin County

Pyke & Merrick: the year the paperwork went quiet

107nights in 2024
0MCF declared in 2024
~3,400kg/hr peak plume

The gap case. XTO declared 2,063–4,330 MCF a year in 2021–23. Then in 2024 — the flare’s biggest year on record, 107 nights and 561 MW — the leases declared zero. Declarations resume in 2025, at 31,181 MCF.

Carbon Mapper plumes up to ~3,400 kg/hr have been imaged around the Pyke and Merrick pads. Burning more than ever in the one year the paperwork goes quiet is hard to read as an oversight.

O’Daniel Ranch: reporting that flickers

25undeclared months

The counter-example that sharpens the rest: HighPeak does declare — up to 260,734 MCF in 2023, among the basin’s biggest declarers — yet still logs 25 sustained months with zero declared while the flare burned up to 276 nights a year.

Across its three sites: 70 undeclared months against only 19 declared. Reporting that switches on and off while the fire doesn’t.

Caveats

Proximity is not ownership: 375 m is a satellite pixel, and a well list is not a deed — though for the named cases a 750 m robustness check and single-operator pixels make misattribution unlikely. The plant exclusion is only as good as the plant lists: R-3 is badly incomplete and EIA-757 locations date to 2017, so every named case was additionally screened against satellite imagery to confirm it sits on lease infrastructure, not a plant. PDQ reports can be amended; “zero declared” means zero in the current RRC extract. The radiant-heat-to-volume conversion is indicative only.

Some undeclared fire is plausibly legal-but-misfiled, with volumes reported under a different lease id than the wells suggest. That is still a data integrity finding — just a different headline.
